User: What is a bug bounty program and how do I start?
AegisX: A bug bounty program pays researchers for finding and reporting vulnerabilities. Platforms like HackerOne, Bugcrowd, and Intigriti connect researchers with companies, and programs may be public, invite-only, or private. To start, learn web fundamentals and OWASP Top 10, then practice on intentionally vulnerable apps like DVWA, OWASP Juice Shop, and PortSwigger's Web Security Academy. Read public reports on HackerOne to learn what good submissions look like. When you are confident, join public programs, read the policy carefully, and start with the lowest-risk in-scope assets. Never test without permission: the policy is your legal boundary.

User: How do I choose a target for bug bounty?
AegisX: Pick programs and assets that match your skill level. Newer applications and APIs tend to have simpler bugs than mature, heavily tested products. Read the scope carefully and prefer assets you can test thoroughly over a huge target list. Look at the program's history: if it accepts many reports and pays consistently, it is worth your time. Consider response time for triage. Focus on one application, map its functionality completely, and test every feature rather than hopping between targets. Depth beats breadth: understanding one application deeply finds bugs that shallow scanning across many targets misses.

User: What is responsible disclosure and how is it different from full disclosure?
AegisX: Responsible disclosure means reporting a vulnerability privately to the owner and giving them time to fix it before any public announcement. The reporter shares enough detail to reproduce the issue, the vendor patches it, and only after a reasonable period, typically 30 to 90 days, is the finding made public. Full disclosure publishes details immediately, often to pressure a vendor that ignores reports. Coordinated disclosure is a middle path where reporter and vendor agree on a timeline together. For bug bounty, responsible disclosure is the norm and usually required by program policy. Publishing before the vendor fixes the issue can get you removed from the program and may be illegal.

User: What should a bug bounty report include?
AegisX: A good report starts with a clear title stating the vulnerability type and affected asset. Give a short summary of the issue and its business impact. Provide step-by-step reproduction instructions with the exact requests and responses, including headers and bodies. Include a proof of concept: a screenshot, or better, a short video showing the impact. Explain why this matters to the business, not just the technical detail. Suggest a concrete fix. Be honest about severity, never exaggerate, and remove sensitive data from any evidence. Reports that are easy to reproduce and show real impact get triaged faster and rewarded better.

User: How do I triage which vulnerabilities matter most?
AegisX: Triage balances impact, exploitability, and asset value. An issue on a login page that lets anyone bypass authentication matters more than a cosmetic bug on a marketing page. Consider what an attacker can do: read other users' data, take over accounts, or execute code? How easy is the exploit: does it need no interaction, one click, or a chain? Use frameworks like CVSS as a starting point, then adjust for business context: what data does the asset hold, and who would be affected? A medium-severity bug on a critical asset can outrank a high-severity bug on a throwaway service. Always validate findings yourself before reporting so triage teams do not reject duplicates or false positives.

User: How do I avoid duplicates when reporting bug bounty findings?
AegisX: Duplicates waste everyone's time, so research before submitting. Search the program's disclosed reports and the duplicate database for similar issues on the same asset. Check whether the exact endpoint or parameter has been reported recently. Test the latest version and current behavior, because a fixed bug that regressed is sometimes treated as a duplicate anyway. Make your report specific: the same generic issue on a different endpoint is usually a duplicate, while a distinct exploitation path with higher impact can be accepted. Report quickly after confirming, because another researcher may find the same bug while you wait. Focus on unique impact angles to differentiate your finding.

User: What is the difference between a vulnerability and an exploit in bug bounty?
AegisX: A vulnerability is a weakness in the system, such as an input that is not validated or a missing access check. An exploit is the technique or code that takes advantage of that weakness to achieve an impact, such as a payload that reads the database. In bug bounty, you report the vulnerability and demonstrate impact with a proof of concept, but you do not need to build a weaponized exploit. Responsible testing stops at proof of impact: show that data can be read or an action can be performed, then stop. Going further, like dumping entire databases or accessing other customers' data, violates the rules of engagement even if the vulnerability is real.

User: How do I test a login flow thoroughly?
AegisX: Map every part of the login flow, not just the form. Test the authentication itself: weak passwords accepted, no rate limiting, and no lockout after repeated failures. Check the reset flow: can you enumerate accounts by the reset response, is the reset token predictable, and does the link expire? Examine session handling: does the session ID change after login, is the session cookie missing HttpOnly or Secure, and does logout actually destroy the session? Test for username enumeration through different error messages. If multifactor authentication exists, try to bypass it by skipping the step, reusing a code, or requesting it for a different session. Each of these is a common finding on real login flows.

User: What is a proof of concept and when is it enough?
AegisX: A proof of concept or PoC demonstrates that a vulnerability is real and shows its impact with minimal risk. For SQL injection, it can be a request that proves the database engine by returning a benign value like the database version, without dumping data. For IDOR, it can be accessing your own second test account's object to show the check is missing, without touching other users' real data. For XSS, a payload that fires an alert or writes to the page is enough to prove execution. A PoC is enough when it shows the vulnerability and its impact clearly and reproducibly. Stop when the impact is proven; continuing to escalate beyond proof can violate scope and turn a legitimate finding into unauthorized access.

User: What tools should a beginner bug bounty hunter learn first?
AegisX: Start with the essentials and add tools as you need them. Learn Burp Suite first because intercepting and modifying requests is the foundation of manual web testing. Add a browser with developer tools for inspecting requests and storage. For content discovery, learn ffuf. For network scanning, learn nmap basics. For subdomain enumeration, use crt.sh and a DNS brute forcer. Learn curl for scripting requests. Practice in labs before touching real targets. Master each tool's core use rather than collecting many tools you barely know. When you understand HTTP deeply, most web bugs become visible with just Burp and a browser.

User: What is a security assessment methodology?
AegisX: A methodology is a repeatable process that ensures you test systematically instead of randomly. A common web assessment flows from reconnaissance to reporting: first enumerate the target's assets and map the application, then analyze each feature for vulnerabilities, exploit only enough to prove impact, and finally write a report with reproduction steps and fixes. Structured methodologies like OWASP WSTG and PTES break testing into phases with checklists, so nothing is forgotten. Following a methodology makes results comparable across engagements and helps you justify every hour. Adapt it to the scope: a quick bug bounty hunt skips full reporting, while a pentest engagement documents everything.

User: How do I prioritize what to test first on a large target?
AegisX: Start where bugs are cheapest to find and most valuable. Begin with authentication and authorization, because flaws there affect every user. Then test input handling on features that accept file uploads, URLs, or rich text, which are historically bug-prone. Look for older or forgotten functionality: staging pages, admin panels, and legacy API versions often lack the security of main flows. Favor features that handle money, PII, or account changes, since impact is what determines severity. Use automated tools to sweep the whole asset while you manually dig into the highest-value areas. Depth on the right features beats breadth everywhere.

User: How do I write a good vulnerability title for a report?
AegisX: A title should let triage understand the issue in one line: the vulnerability type, the affected asset, and the impact. Instead of "IDOR on profile", write "IDOR on GET /api/v1/users/{id} lets any authenticated user read another user's personal data". Include the technique when it matters, like "Stored XSS via profile name leads to account takeover on admin panel". Match the program's naming conventions and avoid vague or sensational words. A precise title sets the tone: triage teams skim many reports, and the title decides whether yours gets opened with interest or filed as unclear. Save the details and proof for the body.

User: What is a 403 bypass and how do I test for it?
AegisX: A 403 response means the server denied access, but many access controls are misconfigured and can be bypassed with variations of the same request. Try different encodings and case: /admin vs /Admin vs /%61dmin. Add headers that some proxies honor: X-Original-URL and X-Rewrite-URL. Try appending characters like a trailing slash, semicolon, or dot, and alternate between /admin and /admin/. Test both /admin and /admin/anything, since some rules match exact paths only. Use different methods: a rule may block GET but allow POST. To test safely, verify you reach the same content a logged-in user would, and confirm the bypass grants access that the control intended to deny.

User: What is the difference between horizontal and vertical privilege escalation?
AegisX: Horizontal privilege escalation means accessing data or functions at the same privilege level you already have, but belonging to someone else: for example, reading another user's profile by changing an ID. Vertical privilege escalation means increasing your privilege, such as a regular user reaching admin functions or an unauthenticated user accessing a protected page. Both come from broken access control, but they differ in what changes: the target object versus the level of access. When testing, look for both: horizontal bugs are found by swapping identifiers between accounts, vertical bugs by probing admin endpoints and role checks as a low-privileged user.

User: How do I set up a local practice lab?
AegisX: A local lab gives you a safe place to learn and test techniques. Install intentionally vulnerable applications: DVWA and OWASP Juice Shop for web vulnerabilities, Metasploitable for a general Linux target, and OWASP Mutillidae for more depth. Run them in Docker or virtual machines so a mistake cannot harm your main system. Add supporting tools in your environment: Burp Suite, ffuf, and nmap. Use the lab to practice the full workflow from reconnaissance to reporting. If you prefer structured practice, platforms like TryHackMe and HackTheBox provide guided vulnerable machines. The goal is to repeat techniques until they are automatic before touching real-world targets.

User: What is an API and how do I test it?
AegisX: An API or Application Programming Interface lets clients exchange data with a server, commonly over HTTP with JSON. To test it, first map it: find the base URL, endpoints, and authentication scheme, often documented at /docs, /swagger, or /openapi.json. Test each endpoint's methods and parameters for the same bugs as web pages: broken object-level authorization by swapping IDs, excessive data exposure in responses, and missing rate limiting. Pay attention to how authentication works, whether it is a key in a header or a token, and test what happens without it. APIs often expose more than the web interface, making them a rich source of bugs.

User: What is the difference between severity and priority in a report?
AegisX: Severity describes the technical impact of a vulnerability if exploited, usually rated with CVSS on a scale from informational to critical. Priority describes how urgently the business should fix it, which also depends on context: a medium-severity bug on the login page of a bank may be higher priority than a high-severity bug on an internal tool nobody uses. When reporting, state the severity honestly using a consistent scale, then explain the business impact so the team can decide priority. Do not inflate severity to get attention; triage teams discount exaggerated reports. Clear, honest assessment builds the trust that makes your reports read carefully.
