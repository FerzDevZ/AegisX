Report writing separates professional testers from script kiddies. A penetration test is only as valuable as its report. Clients pay for findings they can understand, prioritize, and fix. A report that buries critical vulnerabilities in technical jargon fails its purpose. The best reports translate technical detail into business risk.

Every penetration test report opens with an executive summary written for management. The executive summary explains what was tested, what was found, and what it means for the business in plain language. It leads with the headline: how many critical findings, what is the biggest risk, and what should be fixed first. Executives do not need to know the difference between a race condition and an IDOR; they need to know that customer data could be stolen and that fixing it costs less than a breach.

The scope and methodology section documents what the test covered. It lists the in-scope targets, the testing dates, the techniques used, and any limitations. If testing excluded certain systems or ran only during business hours, that goes here. This section protects both sides: the client knows exactly what was tested, and the tester is not blamed for missing systems that were out of scope. Methodology references like OWASP WSTG or PTES show the test followed a recognized standard.

Findings are the heart of the report. Each finding gets its own section with a consistent structure: title, severity, affected asset, vulnerability description, proof of concept, impact, and remediation. The title should be specific: "SQL Injection in login parameter enabling full database read" is better than "SQL injection." Severity comes from the CVSS score combined with business context. A medium-severity vulnerability in an internet-facing system may be more urgent than a high-severity flaw in an internal tool with no data.

The proof of concept makes a finding credible. Include the exact request and response, the payload used, and a screenshot of the result. A triager or developer should be able to reproduce the issue by following the proof step by step. Never include sensitive production data in a report; redact personal information and use placeholder values. For stored vulnerabilities, show the minimal payload that proves the issue without causing harm.

Impact analysis explains why the finding matters. Connect the technical flaw to the business consequence: this SQL injection allows an attacker to read the entire customer database, which violates data protection regulations and could cost millions in fines and lost trust. Impact drives prioritization. A finding with unclear impact gets deprioritized, while a finding with a clear path to account takeover gets fixed immediately. Think like an attacker: what is the worst realistic outcome?

Remediation guidance tells the developer how to fix the issue. Be specific and actionable. For SQL injection, recommend parameterized queries with an example. For missing access control, recommend server-side authorization checks on every request. For weak cryptography, name the algorithm to use and the one to avoid. When a complete fix requires architectural change, provide an interim mitigation that reduces risk while the real fix is planned. Prioritize remediations by severity so the team fixes the dangerous issues first.

Bug bounty reports follow the same structure but shorter. Program triagers read hundreds of reports, so clarity wins. State the vulnerability type in the first line. Provide the endpoint, the exact request, and the response that proves the issue. Explain the impact in one or two sentences: what an attacker could do with this bug. Include the fix suggestion. A clean, complete report gets triaged faster than a vague one. Reputation on bug bounty platforms grows from consistently clear reporting.

Severity assessment requires honest calibration. Beginners inflate severity; experienced testers understate it. Use the CVSS calculator to score the technical severity, then adjust for business context. Self-XSS that only affects the attacker is low severity or out of scope. Account takeover on a banking application is critical. When unsure, ask what the worst realistic impact is. Triagers verify every claim, and overstating impact damages credibility more than understating it.

Remediation verification closes the loop. After the client fixes the findings, retest to confirm the fixes work. Retesting the exact proof of concept confirms the vulnerability is gone. A fix that merely blocks the specific payload while leaving the root cause open will fail retesting with a new payload. The final report update notes which findings are resolved and which remain open. A professional engagement does not end at delivery; it ends when the client is secure.
